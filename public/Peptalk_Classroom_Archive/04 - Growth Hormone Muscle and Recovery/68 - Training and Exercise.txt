What Happens When You Train?

Text saved

Training. Exercising. Working out. Whatever you want to call it. Most of the time, people think it's just moving the body or picking things up and putting them down. But what are you really doing with your body when you're doing these things? 

Here's what's really happening: 

Muscle damage - I know this sounds counterintuitive. But you're creating small microtears in the muscle fibers. Your body is then going to repair those tears, which results in you becoming stronger and more muscular over time. Also why recovery is so important to progress. 

Mechanical tension - this is the force that is being exerted on the muscle that when you lift heavy loads and stretch and contract under tension, can stimulate the muscle cells to begin protein synthesis and build more muscle tissue

Metabolic stress - As you exercise and start to feel the burn, you're going to build up metabolites which can also help trigger the muscle-building responses in the body

Neural adaptation - You are going to get better at recruiting the muscle fibers to work. This is also why you may be able to develop a better mind-muscle connection over time. Your brain and nervous system will become more efficient in recruiting the fibers to work.



Cardio vs. Resistance Training

Text saved

The best thing is a combination of both. You don't have to do one over the other. You won't become bulky from lifting weights. It's actually incredibly hard to look like Arnold, even if you trained hard for 20 years and used steroids. That's why most people don't. Resistance training should be the foundation of what you do. 

Resistance training: 

Builds muscle and strength

Improves metabolic health and insulin sensitivity

Protects bone density (very important as you age!)

Boosts long-term fat loss

Cardio: 

Improves heart and lung health

Aids in recovery and circulation

Helps burn calories

How can you combine both? 

Zone 2 cardio to help with fat burning and recovery

HIIT for some quick, intense, fat burn

Lifting 3-5x per week and 2-3 cardio sessions is a good general guideline for most



Understanding Effort

Text saved

The harsh reality is that most people actually don't workout as hard as they think they do. If you want to see an example of this, watch this video. 

RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion); 

This is a scale of 1-10 for how hard something feels. 

RPE 10 = max effort. Can't do any more.

RPE 8 = that was hard, but I had about 2 reps left in the tank

RPE 6 = relatively good warm-up weight. Not the very first warm-up set, but leading up to the real working sets. 

RIR (Reps in Reverse):

This is how many reps you can still do. 

2 RIR = you could have done about 2 more reps

Intensity doesn't always have to be training until you fall on the floor and collapse. You want to work close enough to your limits that your body has to adapt to that stimulus, but not so much that you can't recover.
