Hair Biology and Growth Cycle Basics

Hair Structure: Your hair grows from follicles in the skin and each follicle is producing the hair shaft which is made of the protein keratin. At the base of the follicle is the bulb and dermal papilla which supply nutrients and signals for growth. On average, we have about 100,000 hair follicles on the scalp, and hair will grow about 0.5 inches (1.25cm) per month. 

Growth Cycle: It is important to note that hair growth isn't continuous. Each follicle cycles through different phases. Anagen is the active growth phase, which for scalp hair will last for about 2-8 years (this is why the head on your hair can grow so long). Around 95-90% of the hairs on a normal scalp are in anagen at any time. The next phase is Catagen and is a short 2-3 week transition where growth stops and the follicle shrinks. Then hair goes through Telogen, which is a period of about 2-4 months where the hair is resting. The last phase, Exogen, is the shedding phase when the resting hair falls out to make room for new growth. When the body is in healthy conditions, the cycle keeps a balance where you shed some hair each day but new hairs are also growing in. It's normal to shed somewhere between 50-100 hairs per day from this cycle. So when you're seeing hairs in the shower, on your pillow, in your brush, etc, they were probably already at the end of their life cycle and ready to fall, you just assisted this process. 

At any given moment you have new hairs coming in, long hairs growing, and old hairs that are resting. That's why losing some hair every day doesn't ultimately result in thinning by itself. The problem really comes when the cycle is disrupted and too many hairs are shedding at once or the growth phase is shortened.



Causes of Hair Loss

Hair loss (known as alopecia) has many possible causes and typically, multiple factors are going to play a role. The main categories are: 

Genetics / Hormonal Causes (Androgenic Alopecia): This is the most common cause of hair loss in both men and women. In men, it's usually called male pattern baldness (MPB) and is linked to hair follicle sensitivity to the androgen hormone DHT (dihydrotestosterone). The follicles that are genetically prone to balding shrink under the influence of DHT which produces thinner hairs and eventually none. This typically results in a receding hairline and thinning at the crown for men. Generally, about 50% of men by age 50 have some level of male pattern hair loss. Women can also have female pattern hair loss which is usually thinning on the crown/top instead of a receding hair line. About 40% of women by age 50 will have noticeable pattern hair loss. Also, genetics from either side can contribute to this (it's not just your grandpa on your mom's side). Hormonal shifts modulate this and many women will see an increase in thinning after menopause since estrogen, which was protecting your hair, decreases. Things like PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) can raise androgen levels and can cause pattern hair thinning in younger women. Ultimately, DHT + genetic susceptibility = follicle miniaturization which is the main culprit in pattern baldness. 




Inflammatory / Autoimmune Causes: Sometimes hair loss occurs outside of genetic reasons. Alopecia areata is an autoimmune condition where the immune system mistakenly attacks your hair follicles which can cause to sudden hair loss. It can happen at any age and both men and women and typically causes you to have bare patches around the size of a coin and can sometimes turn into full hair loss, even on the body. The immune attack forces hair to go into the telogen (shedding) phase prematurely. You also can have scalp inflammation as a contributor to hair loss like severe seborrheic dermatitis or psoriasis. You can also have scarring alopecia where inflammation can permanently destroy the hair follicles, but is pretty rare. For someone with redness, scaling, or pain in the areas where there is hair loss, then they may potentially be experiencing inflammation driven hair loss. Basically, if the immune system or inflammation targets hair follicles, the hair can fall out. 

Nutritional Causes: Deficiencies in certain vitamins and minerals can cause hair to shed or grow poorly. Iron deficiency (especially in women) is a well-known trigger for hair thinning. Vitamin D deficiencies, insufficient protein intake, over restricted calorie intake can all result in hair loss because hair growth is one of the first things the body will shut down if is malnourished. The good thing about this cause of hair loss is that it's usually reversible. Once you fix the deficiency or diet, the hair cycles will normalize themselves over the next few months. Also, unless you have a deficiency, super-dosing these vitamins isn't going to make your hair grow back. 

Stress-Related Causes: When someone is going through a period of high stress, it can shock the hair follicles into a resting/shedding state which is called telogen effluvium. Around 2-3 months after a stressful event, people might notice the shedding occurring. This is another cause that is usually temporary and reversible. Once the body recovers, the follicles resume normal growth and your hair will replenish itself over the next 6 months or so. Chronic stress can lead to longer term periods of telogen effluvium, so managing stress in the long run is important for your hair as well. This also won't typically make you bald but instead will thin out the density. 

Mechanical Causes: Your physical habits can also contribute to hair loss. A great example of this is traction alopecia where persistently tight hairstyles like tight ponytails, braids, man buns, and even a tight hat or helmet can pull on the roots so much that they get damaged. This usually causes thinning at the temples or wherever the tension is highest. It's reversible early on, but if it continues to occur long-term, then it can scar the follicles and ultimately cause permanent loss in those areas. Aggressive hair grooming practices can also contribute like excessive heat styling, chemical treatments, or overbrushing can cause hair breakage and loss. In this scenario, it's not necessarily loss from the follicle, but the broken hairs can make your hair look thinner. 

As you've probably realized from reading this section, hair loss can be a complex situation. It's not always easy to determine one cause. A good general rule of thumb is that if hair loss is a distinct pattern (like receding hairline or crown thinning, genetics/hormones are likely at play. If it's diffuse shedding all of a sudden, consider stress, illness, or deficiency. If it's patchy, consider autoimmune or traction. This leads us into diagnosis (which you sometimes need a doctor's input to really determine).

How do you determine what type of hair loss you have? This is critically important and is half the battle in treating it since they can differ. Here's how this can be approached: 

Assess the Pattern and History: The first thing that should be done is to look at where and how you're losing hair. Pattern hair loss (androgenetic) in men usually presents with a receding hairline and/or thinning at the grown. In women it's more of a general thinning on the top with an intact hairline. If you start to notice a patterned distribution that matches male or female pattern baldness, then it very strongly indicates potential androgenic alopecia. Doctors will typically use the Hamilton-Norwood Scale for men or the Ludwig scale for women to classify the severity of hair loss. Patchy hair loss (like coin-sized patches) can suggest alopecia areata. Diffuse shedding all over suggests telogen effluvium (stress or systemic causes). Another thing to consider is the timing of the loss. Did it come on rapidly over a few weeks (potentially telogen effluvium of alopecia areata), or slowly over years (pattern baldness). Family history can also help to indicate which type as a family pattern of early baldness supports the likelihood of genetic alopecia. Essentially, the pattern + the pace of loss + personal history (like stress events, health, etc) will form a story that can lead to diagnosis. 




Examining the Scalp and Hair: A dermatologist will typically do a close examination on your scalp with something like a dermatoscope or densitometer to look for miniaturized hairs since in pattern hair loss, some follicles shrink and produce fine, wispy hairs before they actually disappear. Seeing variation in hair thickness is a hallmark of androgenetic alopecia. In alopecia areata, you might see short, broken hairs at the patch edges. If there is redness, scaling, or scabs then it could be indicative of an inflammatory or fungal cause. Doctors also might perform a "hair pull test" by gently pulling some bunches of hairs to see how many come out. If someone is shedding more than 5-6 hairs per pull (from multiple spots) then it could suggest active telogen effluvium (excess shedding). They will also check for signs of scarring to see if it's cicatrical alopecia. 




Medical Analysis: Depending on what the suspected cause is, a doctor might have certain tests be done. For something like diffuse hair loss in women, doctors might look at thyroid function, iron levels (ferritin), vitamin D, and hormone levels to rule out deficiencies or thyroid disease since treating those aspects can reverse the hair loss. If the doctor thinks something like an autoimmune disease like lupus is the cause, then they might run labs specific to that. If they aren't completely sure, then a scalp biopsy, where they take a small piece of scalp skin and examine it under a microscope, is the gold standard in determining the cause. Typically, for male of female pattern baldness, the diagnosis is usually pretty clear from the pattern and history. 

Basically if you're having hair loss, then you need to act like a detective. Look at the patterns. Understand the timeline. And look for anything that could have caused this that may have occurred 2-3 months before onset. Forensics (scalp exam and labs) can give further clues. Then you can pinpoint the type of hair loss and develop a targeted treatment plan.



Treatments for Hair Loss

There are numerous potential treatments and interventions when it comes to addressing hair loss. We'll look at FDA-approved treatments first, experimental or supplemental therapies second, and also have some regimens in the next section. 

FDA-Approved and Mainstream Treatments

Right now, there are only two medications officially FDA-approved for treating androgenic alopecia (pattern hair loss) which are Minoxidil and Finasteride. 

Minoxidil: Minoxidil is most commonly used as a topical treatment that you can apply to the scalp. Interestingly, it was first used as an oral blood pressure treatment but people noticed that their hair was growing, so now it's used for that. Minoxidil is a vasodilator, meaning that it widens blood vessels. When applied to the scalp, it's supposed to help increase blood flow to hair follicles and extend the anagen (growth) phase of the hair cycle. It may also directly stimulate the follicle cells and promote thicker hairs. It is pretty effective and slows hair loss for many people who use it and can stimulate new hair growth in some. The clinical trials showed vertex (crown) hair count increases and improvement in density in many users after 4-6 months. It typically works better on smaller, recently thinned follicles than on completely bald areas. Not everyone responds well, but 60+% will see some stabilization or hair regrowth. For men, a 5% solution or foam is typically applied once or twice a day and for women, the 2% twice daily was originally approved, but most women now also use the 5% concentration. Minoxidil has to be used consistently. Think about it like fertilizer for your hair. If you stop, the plant can still wilt away. The same thing happens with minoxidil where the benefits you gained from it might go away. Minoxidil is generally safe and the main side effect is scalp irritation. One important thing to note is that you will shed at the start, typically in the first couple of months, as the old airs are pushed out to make way for new growth. This is the worst / scariest part but is usually a sign that the treatment is working. The studies show that minoxidil can give better results when combined with other therapies. 

Finasteride: Finasteride is an oral prescription medication that is a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor. Basically, it blocks the enzyme that converts testosterone to DHT. By cutting DHT levels (about 60-70% reduction in scalp and blood DHT with the standard 1mg/day dosage), it can remove the hormone that is shrinking the follicles in androgenic alopecia. It has been shown in multiple trials to stop the progression of hair loss in the majority of men, and to even give some regrowth to some. In a 5-year study, about 90% of men on finasteride had no further hair loss and about 65% had moderate regrowth. This is typically dosed at 1mg daily (FDA-approved dose, also achieves near-maximum DHT suppression) and typically takes 6-12 months to see results. Like minoxidil, if you stop using finasteride, you will eventually lose the hair you would have lost. One of the most concerning side effects for men is the sexual side effects. Around 2-4% of men in the trials reported lower libido, erectile dysfunction, or decreased semen volume. This was also similar to the placebo in many studies and typically the side effects improve while taking the medication or reverse after stopping. Some prefer to use finasteride topically to attempt to mitigate systemic issues and decrease the likelihood of side effects. For women, finasteride is not prescribed if they are pre-menopausal as it could cause birth defects, but some post-menopausal women are prescribed it off-label. For men dealing with hair loss, finasteride can work well as it addresses the root cause (DHT) and is very effective at preservation and some regrowth. This is commonly paired with minoxidil for even better results. 

Dutasteride: Dutasteride is basically the stronger version of finasteride. It's a more potent 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor that blocks both type 1 and type 2 5AR enzymes (finasteride is mostly type 2). The results is an even bigger drop in DHT (90%+). The studies have shown that dutasteride can result in more hair growth than finasteride. In a comparison trial, dutasteride increased hair counts significantly more than finasteride (+109 hairs vs +75 hairs in a 1-inch circle). Because dutasteride is a stronger option, it's typically attempted after finasteride doesn't work well enough or if the loss is more aggressive. The side effects are similar to finasteride and it has a longer half-life so side effects may talk longer to resolve after stopping. People also may try this one topically to mitigate or avoid side effects. This one carries risk for women similarly to finasteride. 

Ketoconazole Shampoo: Ketoconazole is an anti-fungal shampoo for dandruff, but is typically used as a supplemental hair loss treatment. It's not FDA-approved for hair loss, but some studies and anecdotal evidence suggest that it can improve scalp health and potentially help with androgenetic alopecia. Ketoconazole has anti-inflammatory properties and can potentially block the androgen receptor and 5AR enzyme in scalp skin. A small study in 1998 found that ketoconazole shampoo used 2-3x weekly was almost as effective as minoxidil in increasing hair density over 6 months. In that study, the minoxidil and ketoconazole groups both had improved hair diameter and percentage of hairs in the anagen phase. Many hair loss physicians will include this shampoo as part of an overall regimen. Use it a couple of times a week in place of your normal shampoo and leave it on for a few minutes then rinse. If you overuse it, it may dry out your hair so don't use it daily unless it's needed for dandruff. 

Topical Anti-Androgens

Systemic DHT blockers like finasteride are effective, but some people may use topical anti-androgens instead to block androgen activity locally within the follicles without significant internal absorption. 

RU-58841: This is a research compound that is quite popular for people as a treatment against hair loss. It is a non-steroidal antiandrogen and binds to the androgen receptors in the hair follicle cells and blocks DHT from binding there. Mechanistically, this makes a lot of sense as you would prevent DHT from docking at the follicle so it is unable to trigger the miniaturization process. In the trials that were conducted, it did show some stimulation of hair growth but the human clinical trials were never published and the compound was never approved. Anecdotally, many people report that it maintains and even regrows hair without systemic side effects. There is no long-term safety data on this compound though, so just understand that. Typically, people apply a 5% solution once daily to the areas they are trying to target. 

Topilutamide: This one is another topical anti-androgen that was developed to selectively break down in the bloodstream so it only stays active on the scalp. It binds to androgen receptors in the scalp and deactivates them, reducing the DHT effects. In studies, they observed an increase in the number of anagen (growing) hairs and a decrease in resting hairs in men with no systemic side effects or hormonal issues. It is typically applied as a 2% solution once nightly to the desired areas of the scalp and is approved as a cosmetic in the EU. Overall it seems to have a pretty good safety profile as well. 

Peptides

Most of the previous treatments revolve around hormones and blood flow, but there are other compounds that may help growth signals and stem cells in the follicles. 

Copper Peptides (GHK-Cu and AHK-Cu): These are popular options that many people use that are interested in peptides. GHK-Cu is a naturally occurring peptide that has regenerative properties in the skin, which translated into hair benefits as well. GHK-Cu can extend the anagen (growth) phase and enlarge hair follicles, making them thicker and growing longer. It is also somewhat anti-inflammatory and can help to protect the follicle cells from damage. AHK-Cu is similar (just a different amino acid sequence) that has shown stimulation of human hair follicle growth in vitro. Both of them are basically signaling the follicles to be in a growth mode. These are generally pretty safe compounds. GHK-Cu is typically used subcutaneous or topically, where AHK-Cu is primarily used topically. 

Zinc Thymulin: This is a combination of a zinc ion with thymulin which has some immunomodulatory effects and may promote anagen (growth phase). They did a study of topical Zinc Thymulin and say increased hair growth over 6 months with no side effects. It may also darken some gray hairs based on that study. The thought process on how this one works is that it is stimulating hair follicle stem cells and counteracting some androgen effects without altering hormones since zinc is beneficial for hair and Thymulin may help encourage the growth phase while calming immune factors. 

PP-405: This one is interesting as it targets the metabolism and stem cell activation in follicles. They realized that when hair follicles are using certain metabolic pathways, it determines if they are active or dormant. PP-405 inhibits the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) in cells which causes metabolic changes that send a signal to dormant hair stem cells and telling them to wake up and enter the growth phase. In a Phase 2a trial, they actually observed new hair growth in areas that were bald, meaning it likely reactivated long-dormant follicles. 21% of those with more advanced baldness had over a 2-% increase in hair density after just 16 weeks of treatment. This one is worth paying attention to as it goes through Phase 3 trials because it could be game-changing in how it works to basically resurrect the miniaturized or dormant follicles.

Here are some ideas of what a protocol might look like, but these can absolutely be changed based on an individuals needs. 

Beginner Protocol (Early or Prevention Focus, Low Risk):

GHK/AHK-Cu: This can be used topically and/or subcutaneously. Subcutaneous administration (GHK-Cu) is typically 1-2.5mg daily. The GHK-Cu or AHK-Cu serum can be applied topically once per day. 

Ketoconazole Shampoo: Apply ketoconazole shampoo on the scalp 2-3x weekly in place of your regular shampoo. 

General Health: Ensure you're getting in enough protein, iron, vitamins, etc. Consider a hair supplement or multivitamin to meet any nutritional gaps. 

Monitoring: Start taking photos of your hair every 3 months in the same lighting / setup so you can track changes. 

Who is this for? Someone who just started noticing a little shedding or thinning or has a family history and doesn't want to move into medications. 

Intermediate Protocol (Moderate Loss or More Aggressive Early Treatment):

Everything in the Beginner Protocol, plus: 

Finasteride: For adult males with pattern hair loss, oral Finasteride at 1mg daily. For post-menopausal women, it would be worth discussing with a doctor for off-label use. 

Minoxidil: Apply once per day to the scalp with a 5% minoxidil solution. Apply this at a separate time of the day from when you apply the GHK-Cu topical. 

Microneedling: At this point, you could introduce something like microneedling with a dermaroller or dermastamp (around 1.5mm depth) on thinning areas once a week. The tiny needles can create micro-injuries that release growth factors and promote hair stem cell activation. 

Who is this for? Someone who has noticeable thinning/balding starting and is trying to halt and try to reverse it. 

Advanced Protocol (Aggressive or Stubborn Hair Loss, Max Regrowth)

All of the above, plus: 

Topical Anti-androgens: Adding in something like RU-58841 can add some localized action on top of these other compounds. Applying a 5% solution once per day (just separate from the other topical compounds) is generally what most users do. 

Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT): Adding in a red light therapy cap or comb 3x per week can increase hair density and growth. This will typically be done in 15-30 minute sessions. 

Who is this for? People with significant hair loss who want to use every method that is scientifically supported to maintain and regrow hair. Understand that the more aggressive the protocol, the more potential for side effects. 

As stated in the beginning, these are examples of what can be done. You may want to avoid finasteride and instead use RU-58841 or PP-405. The ultimate goal is to combine therapies that will compliment each other: an anti-androgen + a growth stimulator + something for scalp health. 

Just remember that any hair regimen requires patience. Hair cycles are slow and you should expect 3 months before seeing less shedding, 6+ months before seeing visible growth, and up to 12-18 months for the most improvement. Consistency is going to be key here.

Think about your scalp like soil where your hair grows. Optimizing the environment of your scalp can improve results from other treatments or even help on its own. 

Microneedling: This is a very popular tool in the hair loss toolkit. Basically, you're using a device with a bunch of tiny needles to create micro-punctures in the scalp. I know it sounds counterintuitive, but doing this causes your body to generate a healing response that releases growth factors which can turn the dormant hair follicles into growth mode. It can also help with the absorption of topical products, so applying something like minoxidil after needling it can penetrate deeper. People will gently roll or stamp (to the point of slight redness) and then apply minoxidil about 24 hours later. This only needs to be done about once per week. It is also important to make sure your tool is sterilized and your scalp is clean so you don't get an infection. 




Red Light Therapy (Low-Level Laser Therapy - LLLT): Red or near-infrared light at specific wavelengths can stimulate hair follicles. Things like laser combs, LED helmets/caps are designed to emit light onto the scalp that penetrates a few millimeters into the skin and is absorbed in cellular mitochondria. This can boost ATP production in the cells and release nitric oxide which leads to improved cell function and vasodilation (blood vessel widening). In hair, this can help to prolong the anagen phase and wake up some telogen follicles to re-enter growth. Multiple studies and a meta-analysis found that these devices increased hair counts significantly vs. placebo in both men and women without serious side effects. This is also incredibly easy to integrate into your schedule as you can put it on your head virtually any time of the day. 

Reduce Traction and Damage: Even outside of traction alopecia, the things you do day-to-day can help your hair. Don't wear extremely tight ponytails or buns frequently. If you start to notice thinning around where you part your hair, try alternating the part line to reduce the stress in one specific area. Doing things like this aren't going to prevent hair loss, but can help control the things that you can control that may cause more issues.
