I wanted to develop this course as a way for people to understand systems of the body so they can better understand how things may be utilized to assist as well understanding how things play into each other and drive different things within the body. 

The immune system is basically your body's defense network against things that can come in and create harm. It's composed of various organs, white blood cells, and chemical signals that all work together to protect our body against things that may cause harm. Some of those things that can cause harm are things like bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, as well as abnormal cells within the body like cancer cells. Basically what the immune system's job is to recognize what ISN'T supposed to be in the body and eliminate it while keeping the healthy cells of the body around. 

The immune system can prevent bad things from coming in to your system, get rid of those that do make it in, and minimize the damage that can be caused by various infections. One thing about the immune system that is incredibly interesting and unique is the ability for it to learn and adapt and essentially "remember" things so it can respond more effectively in the future. 

There are two components to consider when thinking about the immune system which is the innate immune system and the adaptive immune system. The innate immune system is really the first line of defense for the body and will give non-specific protection against a variety of things. This piece of the immune system includes things like skin and mucous membranes, enzymes and acids, and various types of cells. The adaptive immune system is basically like it sounds where it develops immunological memory and helps the body to respond better and faster to future issues with the same pathogens.



Immune Organs and Tissues

Various organs within the body play a role in the immune system's function. The primary lymphoid organs are where immune cells (lymphocytes) are produced and include the bone marrow and the thymus. The bone marrow is a spongy tissue that is found inside the bones and is where most immune system cells are made and multiply. The thymus is a small organ in the upper chest where T cells mature and essentially learn to distinguish the body's proteins from foreign ones. Interesting fact - the thymus will gradually shrink as we go into adulthood and turn to fatty tissue. 

Secondary lymphoid organs are tissues where immune cells are going to do the job of fighting off the bad things that are coming into the body. These organs are the lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, and some mucous membrane layers in the body like in the bowel. The lymph nodes are little bean-shaped groups of tissue that are along the lymphatic vessels and essentially act like filters in the body. Different cells are going to trap germs within the lymph nodes and help produce special antibodies within the blood. So when you're going to the doctor and they check something like your lymph nodes for swelling or pain, they're checking to see that the immune system is active fighting something off essentially. The spleen is going to be within the abdomen and does thing like storing immune system cells to respond via the bloodstream when needed as well as filter germs within the bloodstream, bread down red blood cells, and store and break down platelets. Tonsils are located in the mouth and can help prevent germs coming into the body through the mouth or nose. They have many white blood cells within them to help kill off germs. It's very common for tonsils to be removed so, if that were to happen, the lymphoid tissue on the side of your throat will take over those duties of the palatine tonsils. The last secondary lymphoid organ is going to be the mucous membranes. These are within the intestines, respiratory and urinary tracts, and the lining of the vagina. The gut is probably going to be one of the biggest components of this as more than half of the antibody producing cells are found within the intestine. You also have gut flora which is very important to prevent issues. This is part of why the gut is such an important thing to keep healthy within the body. Immune system cells are going to be located beneath the mucous membranes and help to prevent bacteria and virus growth in those areas.



Immune Cells

Text saved

Just like there are multiple organs and tissues that make up the immune system, there are also multiple different types of cells that make up the immune system. The ones that run around and fight the battles and seek out the enemy so to speak are the white blood cells (WBCs) or leukocytes. These are going to originate in the bone marrow and move throughout the body ready to fight against whatever they need to. There are a lot of different types and each of them serve their own purpose where they can recognize issues, communicate with others, and accomplish the mission. There are a few different types of immune cells that respond differently to those bad things that make their way into the body: 

Phagocytes - These cells are going to essentially eat and digest the pathogens or debris that get into the body. You could picture them as sucker fish that move around the fish tank eating all of the bad things that get in. The phagocytes include neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The neutrophils are the most abundant WBCs within the blood and are very fast to respond to infection or injury sites and eat the bacteria and other bad things that try to make their way in. Macrophages are going to be in tissues throughout the body and come from monocytes that enter the tissues and mature. They also are going to eat pathogens and get rid of bad cells, but also release signaling molecules to bring in other immune cells to the defense lines. Dendritic cells are phagocytes that are going to break down the pathogens that they ingest and then take parts of it (antigens) to the lymph notes. Once they get those antigens to the lymph nodes, they'll show the other immune cells (T cells) what the bad things look like and allow the adaptive immune system to understand what kind of threat they're dealing with. So these are going to be very important in their role as a messenger between the innate and adaptive immune responses within the body. 

Lymphocytes - These cells are going to be central to the adaptive immune system and basically work as commanders coordinating targeted attacks against different pathogens. There are two primary versions of lymphocytes which are B cells and T cells. The B cells are going to mature within the bone marrow (which is why they're called "B" cells) and they are primarily going to produce antibodies. When a B cells recognizes antigens, it can differentiate into a plasma cell that is going to let off a lot of antibodies to either neutralize or mark the pathogens to be destroyed. The T cells are going to mature in the thymus ("T" cells) and come in various forms. There are helper T cells which are going to act like managers within the body and can be activated by an antigen-presenting cells (like dendritic cells) and release cytokines and direct other cells on what to do (like telling B cells to produce antibodies, recruiting phagocytes, etc). Cytotoxic T cells are going to be the killers. They are going to find virus-infected cells or cancerous cells and kill them. These B and T cell lymphocytes are also going to form memory cells after an infection where they are basically on patrol ready to react to the same antigens if they were to show up again. This is important as this memory component allows you to have a lasting immunity after infections or vaccines. 

Natural Killer cells - You'll typically see these abbreviated as NK cells and are a type of lymphocyte that act as a part of the innate immune response. NK cells are going to look for abnormalities within our cells (like if they have some type of virus or are cancerous) and will kill those cells without relying on specific antigens as a trigger. They have toxic granules which are able to punch a hole within cells and cause them to die. They're basically the rapid response killing team that is going to start to put in work before the adaptive T cells are able to get caught up. They don't need prior exposure to something in order to identify it and respond to the threat. 

Granulocytes and other cells - This is a group of white blood cells that have granules with chemicals that they can release to fight against invaders or signal other immune cells. These cells include eosinophils, basophils, and mast cells. Eosinophils are going to fight against larger parasites like worms as well as help with allergic reactions. If you have parasites, eosinophils are goin to release enzymes and toxic proteins from their granules as a way to attack them and get rid of them. They can also contribute to inflammation in things like asthma and allergies. The basophils are going to be a rare circulating granulocyte that, along with the tissue mast cells, are going to orchestrate inflammatory responses by releasing substances like histamine. Mast cells are found in connective tissues and mucous membranes and are going to play a key role in triggering inflammation and are infamous for their role in allergies and anaphylaxis. When they're activated, they're going to rapidly dump histamine and other inflammatory mediators which causes blood vessels to become leaky and call other immune cells to the area. This then leads to the normal allergy symptoms like redness, swelling, and itching but is an important part of the immune response to help recruit other cells to fight at an infection site. 

All of these cells are going to work together to fight issues that your body might encounter. Even something simple like a splinter. So if you get a splinter with bacteria on it, mast cells within the area might trigger inflammation, the neutrophils and macrophages would come in to eat that bacteria, the dendritic cells will take those bacterial antigens and move to a lymph node, activate T cells, then the B cells will produce antibodies, and the NK cells are going to patrol around looking for infected cells. All of these are going to work together in a coordinated effort to eliminate threats that may arrive in the body.



Immune Molecules

Immune cells also rely on various immune molecules to help identify their targets, talk to each other, and get rid of the bad. There are various types that include:

Antibodies - Antibodies (which are also known as immunoglobulins) are Y-shaped proteins that are produced by B cells. They are going to seek out antigens within the body. When B cells turn into plasma cells, they begin to secrete antibodies into the bloodstream and lymphs. An antibody will tightly bind to specific antigens and can directly neutralize a threat or they can mark it for destruction by other immune components. You have different classes of antibodies and each have slightly different roles and locations within the body, but all of them are going to assist with recognizing and helping to eliminate foreign substances within the body. They're going to be very specific in what they do where each antibody is going to fit into one antigen. They'll bind to the antigens and then mark pathogens so phagocytes can more easily ingest them. 

Cytokines - Cytokines are essentially communication signals for the immune system. They are small proteins that immune cells release to influence the behavior of other cells. When your body initiates an immune response, cells start to secrete cytokines to say who should do what and where. Different cytokines are going to have different messages that they send. Cytokines like interleukins are going to mediate communications between white blood cells (leukocytes) to promote activation or growth, interferons help defend against viral infections, chemokines create chemical gradients to attract cells to an infection site. Cytokines ultimately instruct the immune cells to increase or decrease a response, move to a certain spot, produce certain enzymes, and more. 

Complement system - The complement system is a group of proteins in the blood and tissue fluids that work together to fight against invasions and facilitate inflammation when they are activated. They get triggered in a cascade, where each one activates the next when there are pathogens or antibodies bound to pathogens. Once they're activated, they can punch holes in the membranes of bacteria, coat microbes to make them easier to be eat3n by phagocytes, and release different factors that attract immune cells to the area. These are a bridge between the innate and adaptive immune systems and can be activated by pathogen surfaces directly or antibodies that attach to a pathogen. Regardless of how they are activated, they complement the action of the antibodies and cells resulting in a set of membrane attack complexes and tag microbes for cleanup. It also helps to trigger inflammation by causing mast cells to release histamine and recruiting phagocytes. These basically amplify the immune response to help clear infections.



Innate Immunity

Text saved

The innate immune system is basically the rapid response immune system that exists within the body. It's considered "innate" because it's something you are born with and do not require prior exposure to pathogens in order to be effective. Innate immunity includes the physical barriers that were mentioned previously, as well as various cells and molecules that can respond quickly to common features that invaders have. Innate responses are not specific and don't target a certain pathogen by its identity, but instead are going to attack any invader that gets through the walls. Even though it isn't specific, it's important to help control the early stages of an infection and activate the adaptive immune system when it's needed. As an example, this is how your innate immune response is going to look:

The barrier is breached. Let's say you get a cut that gets past your "first line of defense" which is the skin. Once that happens, it allows for bacteria to enter. As soon as that happens, mast cells in the area are going to sense tissue damage and potentially that there are bacteria within the area. Once they happens, they will respond by releasing histamine and other inflammatory responses. Other cells that are close by are going to help to release different signals as well to "sound the alarm" that something is happening. 

Inflammation response occurs. Once histamine is released, the blood vessels within that area start to dilate (get bigger) and start to leak. This is what causes those common signs of inflammation like redness (more blood flow to the area), warmth, swelling (fluids and proteins start to leak into the tissue), and pain (pressure exists on nerves and chemical signals). Even though this type of response is generally uncomfortable, it's helpful as it is going to bring in more immune cells and nutrients and allow for immune proteins and cells to move into the tissue. 

Recruiting other resources. The damages cells and the macrophages and dendritic cells that are within the tissue are going to release cytokines like chemokines to create a chemical trail. The neutrophils and monocytes that are nearby in blood vessels are going to sense these signals and stick to the vessel walls, move out of the vessel and into the infected tissue (called recruitment or extravasation). 

Attack the bacterial invasion. Neutrophils and macrophages will start to phagocytose the bacteria (engulf it) and ingest them. This is going to destroy the pathogens as well as clean up the debris. While they're fighting, the phagocytes can release more cytokines to amplify the response and bring in more backup. If there is a virus infecting cells, they may start to produce interferons to tell the other cells that they need to boost their defenses and natural killer (NK) cells might come in to kill off any infected cells in the area. 

Activate the complement system. The innate immune response will typically trigger the complement system. The complement system will essentially coat the bacteria making them more digestible for phagocytes and form membrane attack complexes to drive holes in the bacterial membranes. They will also help to bring in more immune cells. 

Control the environment. The area of the infection essentially becomes a warzone where they're fighting for control of the area. The body is constantly trying to contain the infection and inflammation will continue until this is resolved. The body is using the inflammatory response to fight off the invasion but also creates a physical barrier via clotting and swelling to wall off the infection essentially. 

Resolution or escalation. Typically, the invasion can be eliminated within a few days when the body has a successful innate response and the inflammation will start to go away. Macrophages move towards healing and will start to clear out dead cells and help with tissue repair. If the pathogen isn't able to be contained, the innate system has essentially bought time and signaled for help from the other immune processes and systems the body has. The dendritic cells that engulfed bacteria will move through the lymphatic vessels to the nearest lymph node and present antigens from the microbe to T cells and activate the adaptive immune system which may take a couple of days to kick in. 

This shows what is really happening and the key component of the innate immune system which is a rapid response, inflammation, phagocytosis and generalized attack. The innate immune system isn't concerned with the type of bacteria, but instead looks at common features like bacterial cell wall components to respond and attack. This innate system does not have any type of memory of the pathogen and will respond the same way regardless. Innate immunity can usually get rid of minor things on its own and is important in activating and shaping how the adaptive immune response will look if a stronger, more specific attack is needed.



Adaptive Immunity

Text saved

The adaptive immune system is going to act as the next line of defense and takes a little longer to start working, but also works very specifically and has a memory. Innate immunity is going to broadly attack where adaptive immunity is going to tailor that attack to the specific invasion after learning its characteristics. Adaptive responses get executed by lymphocytes (mostly B and T cells) and the antibodies and other molecules that they produce. The system is known as the adaptive system because it adapts to the invaders and each time a new pathogen is encountered, they conduct a specialized response and remember the pathogen in the future in case they ever encounter it again. 

The important pieces of the adaptive immune system are that they are antigen-specific, they are systemic and can mobilize all defenses, and it has memory. But with this, it is slower to start working and usually doesn't really kick in and become effective until a few days after the initial infection starts as it takes some time for the appropriate lymphocytes to be active and expand. This is how adaptive immunity works: 

Antigen recognition - An antigen is a molecule or piece of a pathogen that the immune cells can recognize which is the cornerstone of the adaptive immune system as it has to be able to recognize these. Each B and T cell have receptors that can bind to specific antigen shapes. Before exposure, the body is going to hold a lot of B and T cells with random specificities that are generated in their development. Once an infection occurs, the antigen-presenting cells are going to take some pieces of the pathogen to the lymph nodes or spleen and present the antigen to T cells that haven't yet encountered their specific antigen. This will activate a matching T cell to respond. They can also be recognized by B cells and typically use the help of a T cell to do so. 

Clonal expansion - Once a B or T cell is activated by a specific antigen, it going to undergo a process called clonal expansion where it proliferates and makes thousands of clones of itself that target the same antigen. Once they've cloned themselves, they differentiate into effector cells (ones that carry out an immune response) and memory cells that stay long-term. 

Getting rid of the pathogen - Effector T cells are going to move from the lymph node to the infection site where helper T cells are going to secrete cytokines to boost local immune response. Cytotoxic T cells are going to look for cells that display the antigen they are looking for, bind to them, and kill those cells, typically through apoptosis. 

Resolution - Once the immune system wins the fight, the immune response is going to calm itself back down. The effector cells are going to die off or reduce down, which is important to avoid having too much damage or an ongoing immune response that is unnecessary. The memory cells are going to stay in the body and make sure that if the same thing tries to make its way in, your body will be able to have a stronger and faster response to it. Because of this, typically if you have a vaccine or illness you have either immunity or are much better protected against them since your body knows and understands how it should respond.
