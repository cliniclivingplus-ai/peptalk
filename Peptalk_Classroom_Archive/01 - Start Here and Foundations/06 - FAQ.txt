Should SS-31 be taken before MOTS-C, or the other way around, and what's the best general protocol for improving mitochondria?asked 4×

My position is SS-31 first. SS-31 stabilizes cardiolipin in the mitochondrial membranes and improves structural efficiency, while MOTS-C increases metabolic stress signaling and energy demand. You want to fix the damaged mitochondria first, then increase demand on it — stabilize the structure with SS-31, then optimize function with MOTS-C. That sequencing makes more physiological sense than the reverse, and it's the overwhelming consensus. In practice, you can either run SS-31 for several days before introducing MOTS-C, or finish the full 50mg total run of SS-31 first and then start MOTS-C — SS-31 is typically run until that total 50mg is completed, and MOTS-C is commonly run 4–6 weeks on. People tend to overcomplicate mitochondrial support, but for most people SS-31 followed by MOTS-C is going to be more than sufficient — start there before adding anything else. If you're also on NAD+, you can generally continue it alongside both unless you're feeling overstimulated or overly wired; there's no hard requirement to cycle NAD+, but if you've been on it 3 months straight, a short break wouldn't be unreasonable.

Should I run MOTS-C daily at a lower dose or every other day at a higher dose, and is it okay to split the standard 5mg dose into smaller amounts?asked 4×

Trying lower is fine — some people respond well at lower amounts, and some have to work up higher.

Is it worth going all the way up to the highest dose of Retatrutide (12mg) for maximum weight loss?

Looking at the clinical trial data, going from 8mg up to 12mg only adds about 2.5% additional body weight loss over a year - for a 200-pound person that's roughly 5 extra pounds. That small gain comes with about a 50% increase in the amount of compound you're using and a much higher incidence of side effects like nausea. Starting at 2mg and titrating up to 8mg actually offers a better risk-reward profile than starting at 4mg and pushing to 12mg. For a marginal extra weight loss of around 0.1 pound per week, the increased cost and side effects of the highest dose just aren't justified for most people. Higher doses don't always yield proportionally better outcomes, and most of the weight loss happens in the initial weeks even at lower doses.

How do different red light therapy devices (heat lamps, LED panels, mats, beds) compare in effectiveness?asked 2×from the feed

Beds (full-body red light beds) — the move for whole-body coverage and convenience, like general recovery or skin. Tradeoff: usually milder per area and expensive/commercial, so you're paying for the full-body convenience.

Does the mg amount on a COA reflect a specific vial or the batch average, and how much vial-to-vial or batch-to-batch variation is normal/acceptable?asked 5×from the feed

The mg amount on a COA only reflects the vial (or the handful of vials) that actually got sent in for testing - it is not a true batch average. Labs are only able to test what's in front of them. They could test one vial, or a company could send a few, but the only way to truly know the average across an entire batch would be to test every single vial in it, and that would mean destroying the whole batch to verify it. So realistically you're looking at data from 1-3 vials representing a batch that could be dozens or hundreds of vials. It doesn't really show you what it'll truly be across the whole batch.

As for how much vial-to-vial or batch-to-batch variation is 'normal' - I don't have a number to give you on that, and honestly nobody can promise you one, because a COA doesn't even test for what the compound is the same way a purity test does. The sterility and purity tests are testing the sample they were given, tied to a batch number. That's why trust matters so much: a bad manufacturer could test one good vial they know is clean, then send you whatever they want from the rest of the batch, or swap caps to make things look consistent. Good companies combat this by testing multiple times - the manufacturer tests the batch, then the company retests when they receive it - and they'll let you send a vial out for independent testing too, usually with a refund clause if it comes back bad. But truthfully, you never fully know that what you received matches what's on the COA. That's why I always say never blindly trust a COA - use it as a layer of assurance, not proof.

How should you interpret endotoxin readings on a COA (units, high values, and general concern level)?asked 3×from the feed

First thing: endotoxin is a separate safety test — most basic COAs don't even include it.

Your standard COA is testing identity (is it the right compound, by mass spec) and purity (by HPLC). Endotoxin is an extra test beyond that bare minimum. So if a vendor is running endotoxin testing at all, that's already a good sign they care.

What it is: endotoxin is basically bacterial junk left behind from certain bacteria's cell walls. It's measured in EU/mg — endotoxin units per milligram. This is the stuff that causes pyrogenic reactions — fever, chills, flu-like feeling, nasty injection-site reactions — even when the compound itself is pure. That's the key point: a peptide can test high purity and still make you feel like garbage if the endotoxin is high, because purity and endotoxin are two different things.

How to read it: lower is better, and "not detected" or below the reporting limit is ideal. Here's how to actually think about the number — the accepted medical standard is 5 EU per kg of bodyweight per hour. So a 100kg person could technically handle up to 500 EU in an hour and still be within the medical limit. Obviously we're not trying to hit that ceiling; you want it as low as possible. But to put it in perspective, something like 20 EU in a full vial isn't anything I'd be majorly concerned about. It's when a number is clearly elevated well beyond that that I'd steer clear.

And the usual rules still apply: never blindly trust a vendor's own COA, you want third-party testing, and the unique key needs to be there so you can actually verify it.

Should the amount of visible powder/liquid in a vial always match the labeled dose, and why might it differ between vendors?asked 2×from the feed

Short answer: no — the amount of powder you see does NOT have to match the dose, and it varies vendor to vendor for totally normal reasons.

Here's what trips people up: peptides come as a freeze-dried (lyophilized) powder, and how much powder you see has almost nothing to do with how many mg are actually in there. What matters is the labeled mg, and you verify that with the COA (the quantity test) — not by eyeballing the vial.

Why it looks different from vial to vial and vendor to vendor:

The freeze-drying process changes how the powder settles — sometimes a big fluffy cake, sometimes a puck at the bottom, sometimes just a thin film or a tiny speck that makes the vial look basically empty.

Some manufacturers add a small amount of bulking agent (like mannitol), which makes the cake look bigger; others don't — so the same 5mg can look like way more powder from one vendor than another.

Shipping shifts it around too — the powder scatters up the sides of the vial or looks like less than you expected.

So a vial that looks "half empty" or barely has a crumb in the bottom is usually totally normal. Don't panic when your 5mg vial looks nearly empty.

What actually matters:

The mg on the label, confirmed by the COA.

Once you reconstitute, it's the math that counts — how much bac water you added determines your mg per unit. A tiny-looking amount of powder dissolved into your bac water is still the full dose.

When would I question it? Only if something's dramatically off from what you'd expect for that vendor and vial. Visual differences between vendors on their own are not a red flag.

How important is it to monitor IGF-1 levels while on a GH secretagogue like Tesamorelin or CJC-1295, and is there a long-term safety concern?asked 2×from the feed

I do think it matters, and here's why. Secretagogues like Tesamorelin or CJC-1295 are working through your own feedback systems (somatostatin, pituitary responsiveness, etc.), so they're going to run into a ceiling - you'll typically see IGF-1 rise into the high-normal range, not way beyond it like you can push with high-dose exogenous GH. How accurate is the Hume Body/Health Pod compared to a DEXA scan for body composition metrics?asked 2×from the feed

Quick version: DEXA is the more accurate number; the Hume Pod is better for tracking trends. Use them for different jobs.

DEXA scan — the gold standard for body composition. A quick, low-dose X-ray that breaks down fat mass, lean mass, bone density, and where you hold it regionally. If you want a true, accurate number, DEXA is it. Downside: it costs money each time and isn't something you do every week.

Hume Body Pod — a smart home device that estimates body fat using bioimpedance (a tiny electrical current) plus their modeling. Upside is convenience — measure at home as often as you want. Tradeoff: less accurate on the absolute number, and sensitive to hydration, food, and time of day.

Here's the key thing people mess up: don't expect the Hume number to match your DEXA number. Bioimpedance can read a few percent off DEXA in either direction, so comparing them head-to-head will just drive you crazy — they measure differently.

How I'd actually use them:

Use a DEXA every few months as your accurate checkpoint / true baseline.

Use the Hume in between to track the trend — same time of day, same conditions (ideally fasted, morning, before training) so it's consistent.

Watch the direction on the Hume, not the exact number. Trending down over weeks means you're on track; confirm the real number at your next DEXA.

Bottom line: DEXA for accuracy, Hume for frequency. They're complementary, not competitors — just don't treat the two numbers as interchangeable.

But, if you want compounds that work on different receptors / pathways, GH secretagogues along with a calorie deficit, MOTS-C, etc can work. If appetite suppression is needed, Cagrilintide is really the only other option for this that may work, but remember it is crazy strong and could suppress your appetite too much. However, the way in which it works in the body is different so could maybe be an option for true non-responders. 

Also, before classifying yourself as a non-responder, make sure calories, nutrition, and exercise are in check. This includes eating too little. 

I left my peptide sitting out on the counter. Is it still good? 

Yes. It is still good. Even if you left it out for a few days, everything will likely be fine. Just don't make a habit of it. 

Does the size / weight of a person matter when it comes to dosages? 

Yes and no. For a lot of these, there are generally going to be ranges that are effective regardless of bodyweight. However, especially when tested in studies, some things might have been done as a relative amount to bodyweight. Generally speaking though, they'll be done within an effective range regardless of size. 

Do I need to inject BPC/TB/GLOW/KLOW at the site of injury? 

No. They will work systemically regardless of where they are injected. Now, if my shoulder hurts I'll go in my shoulder since it's a site I would inject anyways. But I wouldn't inject in my groin or my ankle because it's just not necessary. 

Should I increase dosage or stack with another peptide? 

I'm assuming this question is most related to losing weight. The true answer is that either could work. If you can accomplish 2 goals by adding another peptide, that may be the better option. However, before adding or increasing, I would check nutrition with tdeecalculator.net and activity levels first, then go from there. 

Do peptides create dependencies? 

No... and yes. They aren't going to create true dependencies like things that you will become "addicted" to, but you may like how it makes you feel or the improvements you may get. This does create a potential dependency feeling which is why I say the "and yes" in my answer. 

What peptides do you need to cycle off and for how long? 

I am always going to be a proponent for cycling off and taking breaks. The Peptide Cycles course will answer all of this for you on what I think may be best :)
